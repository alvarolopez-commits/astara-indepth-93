Whales, eels, cyclists, robots, ships and drones on one energy scale

Prepared 3 November 2026 COT from 0.003 to 16
00Summary

There is no single most efficient mover

There is no single most efficient mover. There is a winner for each thing you choose to count, and the winners share three traits: they are big, slow and supported by water, rails or a smooth road.

Among living beings, the large swimmers win. A humpback whale cruising at its best speed has a cost of transport of about 0.0031, by an estimate from breathing rates; a European eel, measured in a swim tunnel, about 0.060. A fast-walking human spends 0.387, roughly 6 times the eel. Size matters as much as medium: per kilogram, a mouse-sized runner spends 47 times more than an elephant-sized one.

The bicycle is still the best way for a human body to move itself: 29 Wh of food energy per km against 72 Wh for walking. But machines built only for efficiency go much further. A student prototype at the 2026 Shell Eco-marathon covered 538 km on 1 kWh: 1.9 Wh per km, a cost of 0.0091 per kg of driver, about 16 times less than a cyclist.

For cargo, the champion is the ship: 0.014 per kg of freight, about half the cost of a freight train and 1,176 times less than a small delivery drone. Robots are the laggards: the best humanoids still spend about 10 times more energy per metre than a walking person.

From the analysis
01The Yardstick

One number for a whale and a ship

Engineers Gabrielli and von Karman asked in 1950 what speed costs in energy; biologist Vance Tucker answered in 1975 with a chart that set fruit flies, salmon, horses, jets and a man on a bicycle on one line. Their trick was to divide energy by weight and distance, which removes the units.

The same formula is used here for every row. Many sources publish it directly; for the rest it is computed from energy per kilometre and mass, or from power, mass and speed. Each row shows its inputs.

Yardstick

The yardstick is the cost of transport (COT): the energy used to move a weight over a distance, divided by that weight and that distance. It has no units, so a whale, a bicycle and a ship sit on the same axis. A COT of 0.1 means that moving 1 kg one metre costs as much energy as lifting it 10 cm.

Two bases

Two bases, never mixed in one contest: "own mass" (the animal, robot or whole vehicle moving itself) and "payload" (per kg of passenger or cargo carried; a passenger counts as 75 kg, the EU type-approval driver mass).

Energy boundary

Energy boundary: living beings and human power are measured in food (metabolic) energy; machines in electricity or fuel delivered to the vehicle. Farming, power plants and refineries are not counted anywhere.

Sources

Sources: every figure is external, and every source is listed with its URL at the end of the page. Where a source gives energy and mass but not COT, the COT is computed and the inputs are shown. Nothing is estimated; "ND" means no defensible figure.

02Living Beings

Living beings: big swimmers win

  • Humpback whale: 0.0020-0.0042, derived from breathing rates at the optimal speed of 2-4 m/s off Peru. It is the lowest number in the piece and the least direct.
  • European eel: 0.051-0.069, measured in swim tunnels simulating the 5,500 km migration to the Sargasso Sea. Eels swim 4-6 times more efficiently than salmon-like fish.
  • Beluga whale: 0.143, measured by oxygen use in an aquarium.
  • Moon jellyfish: reported as the cheapest swimmer in its comparison, with fish only beating it above about 100 kg; its body is mostly water, which flatters a per-kg metric. No number is published in the text (ND).
  • Birds: fliers sit between swimmers and runners; a sparrow's minimum cost is about ten times lower than a mouse's, because it covers ground ten times faster for the same metabolic rate. No comparable bird number is included.

Size matters as much as medium. A regression across 62 running birds and mammals gives a net cost of 3.76 for a 20 g runner, 0.285 for a 70 kg one and 0.079 for a 4,000 kg one.

03The Human Body

The human body and its best machine

On a bicycle, a person needs 29 Wh of food energy per km, against about 72 Wh on foot: well under half. Muscles still lose about three quarters of food energy as heat, which is why an electric scooter, at 15.9 Wh per km of electricity, looks cheaper still.

04Robots

Robots: still far from biology

The record holder is the Cornell Ranger, which walked 65 km in 2011 on one charge at 0.280, close to a walking human, but it can do nothing except walk. The bipedal Cassie reaches 0.680. Full humanoids sit at 2.27 (ASIMO) to 2.80 (WALK-MAN), ten or more times a walking person, and the hydraulic BigDog at 15.0.

The new generation of commercial humanoids cannot be placed on this scale yet. Fraunhofer IPA's 2026 benchmark of the Unitree G1 publishes battery life (2 h 49 min standing, 1 h 49 min standing and walking) but not the distance or speed, so its cost of transport is ND.

05Moving People

Moving people

At the top, the Eco-marathon prototype (1.9 Wh per km) shows the physical limit for a one-seat vehicle on a smooth track. Among things people actually use, the shared e-scooter (15.9 Wh) and the full electric bus (about 44 Wh per passenger at 40 on board) lead. A battery-electric car carrying one person uses 210 Wh, and the average passenger-km on British roads 395 Wh, about 213 times the prototype.

06Moving Goods

Moving goods

A ship moves a tonne one kilometre with about 38 Wh, a freight train with 67 Wh, an average truck with 608 Wh and a cargo aircraft with 4,556 Wh. A small drone carrying a 0.5 kg parcel spends 44,440 Wh per tonne-km. Per parcel on a short trip it can still beat a van, but per kilogram it is the costliest mover in the piece.

Seen as a whole vehicle, a 40-tonne electric truck in a design model reaches 0.0098, a number close to the ship's per-kg figure and lower than any land animal.

07The Verdict

So, who wins?

  1. 01
    Living beings

    If the question is "which living being moves its own body most cheaply?": the large swimmers. The humpback whale by estimate (0.0020-0.0042); the eel by measurement (0.051-0.069).

  2. 02
    Moving a person

    If it is "which way of moving a person uses the least energy?": a purpose-built prototype (0.0091 per kg of driver). Among everyday options, the e-scooter on electricity and the bicycle on food energy.

  3. 03
    Moving goods

    If it is "which way of moving goods uses the least energy?": the ship (0.014 per kg of cargo), then the freight train.

  4. 04
    Walking robots

    If it is "which machine walks like us?": none yet. The most efficient walking robot matches a human only because it does nothing else.

08What to Watch

What to watch

  1. 01
    Humanoids · Fraunhofer IPA

    Humanoid benchmarks: Fraunhofer IPA has published a method rather than results; the first published distance and speed for a commercial humanoid will allow a real cost of transport.

  2. 02
    Electric trucks and ships

    Electric trucks and ships: long-haul electric trucking scenarios already sit at about three times the ship's per-kg cost of moving cargo; fleet data will test that.

  3. 03
    Bird flight · FLIGHT project

    Bird flight: wild-flight tagging studies (e.g. the Swansea FLIGHT project, 46 species) promise the first comparable field costs of transport for fliers.
